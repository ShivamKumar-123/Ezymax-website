Why did gold jump 30 dollars in an hour? Why did the Nasdaq fall on a day when the economy looked strong? Beginners often look for a single cause, but prices move for one reason at the most basic level: the balance between buyers and sellers changed. The useful question is what changed that balance. This chapter introduces the main forces, which later phases examine in depth.

## Supply, demand and expectations

At any moment the price is where buyers and sellers agree. When new information makes an asset seem more valuable, buyers become willing to pay more and sellers ask more, so the price rises until the balance returns. The key word is *new*. Markets are forward-looking: large participants spend enormous resources forecasting interest rates, earnings and economic data, and they trade on those forecasts in advance.

That means much of what is expected is already **priced in**. By the time a widely forecast event happens, the price may already reflect it. What moves the market is the difference between what happens and what was expected, the **surprise**.

> **Example:** US consumer inflation (CPI) is forecast at 3.1% year on year. It is released at 3.4%. Higher-than-expected inflation makes higher US interest rates more likely, so the dollar strengthens. EURUSD drops from 1.0850 to 1.0790 within an hour, a fall of 0.0060, or 60 pips. If CPI had come in exactly at 3.1%, the same "high inflation" would have produced little reaction, because it was already in the price.

## The main drivers

Across the instruments on Kalks, a handful of forces explain most large moves.

**Interest rates and central banks.** Money tends to flow toward currencies where the expected return is higher. When a central bank is expected to raise rates, its currency usually strengthens; when cuts are expected, it usually weakens. Rates also affect gold, which pays no interest, and equities, whose future earnings are worth less when rates rise.

**Economic data.** Employment, inflation, growth and business surveys change expectations for rates and earnings. Phase 3 explains each release and Phase 4 shows how to read the economic calendar.

**Risk sentiment.** When investors feel confident, they buy equities and higher-yielding currencies such as AUD. When they are fearful, they often move into perceived safe havens such as the US dollar, Japanese yen, Swiss franc and gold. This shift, called risk-on and risk-off, can move many instruments at once.

**Supply shocks.** Commodities respond directly to physical supply. An unexpected cut in oil output, a pipeline disruption or conflict in a producing region can move USOIL and UKOIL sharply.

**Company news.** Individual shares move on earnings, guidance, product news and regulation. Large companies such as NVDA or AAPL also move the indices they belong to.

**Flows and positioning.** Large transactions, such as month-end portfolio rebalancing or a corporation converting a big payment, can move prices without any news. If most traders already hold the same position, there are few new buyers left, and even good news can cause a drop as they take profits.

## One event, many markets

Because instruments share drivers, a single release ripples across the platform. Suppose US data suggests rates will stay higher for longer:

| Instrument | Typical first reaction | Why |
|---|---|---|
| EURUSD | Falls | The dollar strengthens |
| USDJPY | Rises | The dollar strengthens against the yen |
| XAUUSD | Falls | Higher yields make non-yielding gold less attractive |
| NAS100 | Falls | Higher rates reduce the value of future earnings |
| BTCUSD | Often falls | Tighter financial conditions weigh on speculative assets |

These are tendencies, not rules. Relationships change over time, and on a given day another driver can dominate. Phase 5 covers these intermarket links properly.

## When the reaction surprises you

Sometimes good news produces a falling price. Usually one of three things is happening:

1. **The news was better, but not as good as hoped.** A figure above last month can still be below the forecast.
2. **Positioning.** Traders bought in advance and now sell to lock in profits.
3. **Something else matters more.** A strong jobs report might be outweighed by a weaker wage figure in the same release.

Watching how a price reacts to news is therefore informative. A market that cannot rally on good news is often weaker than it looks.

> **Risk warning:** Prices can move very fast around news, spreads widen and orders may fill at worse prices than requested. Trading leveraged CFDs through major releases can produce losses larger than planned. News trading is covered in Phase 4; until then, be cautious around high-impact events.

## Common mistakes

- **Reading headlines instead of surprises.** "Inflation is high" is not tradable information if everyone already expected it.
- **Assuming one cause for every move.** Markets weigh many factors at once; the explanation offered afterwards is often simpler than reality.
- **Ignoring shared drivers.** Being long gold and long NAS100 can look diversified, but both may fall on the same hawkish rates surprise.
