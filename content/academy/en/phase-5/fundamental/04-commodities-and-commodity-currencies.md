Some countries earn a large share of their national income by selling raw materials abroad. Their currencies, known as **commodity currencies**, tend to rise and fall with the prices of those exports. Understanding the link helps you read AUDUSD and USDCAD, and it gives you a second market to check before committing to a trade.

## Why commodity prices move currencies

When the price of a country's main export rises, three things tend to happen:

1. **More foreign currency flows in.** Exporters receive more dollars for the same volume and convert them into the local currency to pay wages, taxes and suppliers. That conversion is demand for the local currency.
2. **Terms of trade improve.** The terms of trade are the ratio of export prices to import prices. Better terms of trade raise national income and usually support the currency.
3. **Rates can be affected.** Stronger income and investment in the resource sector can lift growth and inflation, which may lead the central bank to keep rates higher.

The reverse applies when export prices fall. The link runs mainly from commodity to currency, but it is a tendency that other drivers can override.

## The main commodity currencies

| Currency | Key exports and links | Useful things to watch |
|---|---|---|
| Australian dollar (AUD) | Iron ore, coal, LNG, gold; China is the largest customer | Iron ore prices, Chinese PMIs and credit data, RBA policy |
| Canadian dollar (CAD) | Crude oil, natural gas; the US takes most exports | USOIL, US growth data, Bank of Canada policy |
| New Zealand dollar (NZD) | Dairy, meat, agriculture | Dairy auction results, Chinese demand |
| Norwegian krone (NOK) | Oil and gas | Brent crude (UKOIL), Norges Bank policy |

On Ezymex you can trade the first two directly through AUDUSD and USDCAD. Note the quoting: in USDCAD the Canadian dollar is the quote currency, so a **stronger CAD means USDCAD falls**. A beginner who expects "oil up, USDCAD up" has the relationship backwards.

## AUD and China

Australia ships most of its iron ore to China, where it feeds steel production for construction and infrastructure. As a result, AUDUSD often reacts to Chinese data as much as to Australian data. A strong Chinese manufacturing PMI, new stimulus for the property sector or a rally in iron ore can support AUD; signs of a Chinese slowdown tend to weigh on it.

AUD is also a **risk-sensitive** currency. Because it is linked to global growth and has historically offered higher yields than JPY or CHF, it tends to fall when investors become risk-averse. This makes AUDJPY a popular barometer of global risk appetite, a theme you will meet again in Phase 6.

## CAD and oil

Crude oil is Canada's largest export, and most of it goes to the United States. When oil rallies, CAD tends to firm and USDCAD tends to drift lower.

> **Example:** USOIL rises from 78.00 to 84.00 over two weeks, a gain of about 7.7%, after producers announce output cuts. Over the same period, with Canadian and US rate expectations steady, USDCAD falls from 1.3650 to 1.3520, a drop of about 0.95%. The currency moves far less than the commodity, which is normal: oil is only one of many influences on the Canadian economy.

Two points from the example matter for trading. First, the currency moves much less than the commodity, so a big oil move does not translate into a big USDCAD move. Second, because Canada sells most of its oil to the United States, CAD is also tied to US growth. A US slowdown can hurt CAD through lower export volumes even if oil prices hold up.

## Checking that the link is working

Correlations between commodities and currencies drift. In some years the oil-CAD link is strong; in others, interest-rate differentials dominate completely. Before relying on it:

1. Compare the two charts over the last 20 to 60 daily candles. Are the swings broadly aligned (inversely, for USDCAD)?
2. Check the interest-rate story. If the two central banks are moving in different directions, that may dominate.
3. Note any one-off factors, such as a pipeline outage, a local election or a trade dispute, that could decouple the two.

## Common mistakes

- **Getting the quote direction wrong.** For USDCAD, CAD strength shows as a falling chart.
- **Trading the currency as a leveraged proxy for the commodity.** If your view is on oil, trade USOIL or UKOIL; if your view is on Canada, trade CAD. Mixing the two adds noise.
- **Assuming the link is fixed.** A relationship that worked last year may not work this quarter.
- **Doubling exposure unknowingly.** Long USOIL and short USDCAD are, in many periods, largely the same bet. Size them together as one position, as explained in the technical chapter on correlation.

> **Risk warning:** Commodity markets can gap on supply news and weekend events. CFDs are leveraged, so a gap through your stop can produce a loss larger than planned.
