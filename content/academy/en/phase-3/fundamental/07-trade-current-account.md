Interest rates, inflation, growth and fiscal policy describe an economy from the inside. Trade and the balance of payments describe how it deals with the rest of the world. Every time a country buys foreign goods, earns income abroad or attracts foreign investment, currencies change hands. Over months and years, these flows help explain why some currencies trend and why some fall hardest when markets panic.

## The balance of payments

The balance of payments records all transactions between a country and the rest of the world. It has two main parts.

The **current account** includes:

- The **trade balance** in goods (cars, oil, electronics) and services (tourism, finance, software).
- **Primary income**: investment income such as dividends and interest earned on foreign assets, minus what is paid to foreign investors.
- **Secondary income**: transfers such as remittances by workers sending money home and foreign aid.

The **capital and financial account** records investment flows: foreign direct investment such as building a factory, purchases of shares and bonds, bank lending and changes in central-bank reserves.

By accounting identity, the two sides balance, apart from errors and omissions. A country with a current-account **deficit** must have an equal net inflow on the financial account. In plain terms: if a country spends more abroad than it earns, foreigners must be lending to it or buying its assets to fill the gap.

## Why the current account matters for currencies

A deficit is not automatically bad. Fast-growing economies often import capital to fund investment. The issue is **dependence**. A country running a deficit of 4% or 5% of GDP relies on continuous foreign inflows. While investors are happy, the currency can stay strong. When sentiment turns, those inflows can slow or reverse, and the currency often has to weaken to restore balance, either by making assets cheaper for foreign buyers or by making imports more expensive.

> **Example:** A country has GDP of 2,000 billion USD and a current-account deficit of 80 billion USD, or 80 / 2,000 = 4% of GDP. In a calm year, foreign purchases of its bonds and equities cover the gap. When a global sell-off hits and foreign investors pull back, the currency weakens sharply against safe havens. Another country with the same interest rate but a 3% current-account surplus usually falls much less in the same episode, because it does not need foreign money.

Surplus countries tend to have the opposite profile. Japan and Switzerland run persistent current-account surpluses and hold large net foreign assets. In a crisis, their investors often bring money home, which is part of why JPY and CHF behave as safe havens.

The US is a special case. It has run a current-account deficit for decades, yet the dollar remains the world's main reserve currency because global demand for dollar assets, especially Treasuries, is so strong.

## Terms of trade and commodity currencies

The **terms of trade** compare a country's export prices with its import prices. When export prices rise relative to import prices, the country earns more for the same volume of goods, which tends to support its currency.

This is the backbone of the commodity currencies:

- **AUD**: iron ore, coal, natural gas and gold.
- **CAD**: crude oil, a major export mostly sold to the US.
- **NZD**: dairy and agricultural products.
- **NOK**: oil and gas.

A sustained rise in crude oil prices (USOIL, UKOIL) tends to help CAD, so USDCAD often moves inversely with oil over longer periods. The relationship is not reliable on an intraday basis, but it is a useful background factor. Commodity currencies are covered further in Phase 5.

## Trade policy and tariffs

Tariffs, export controls and trade deals change trade flows and can move markets quickly. Tariff announcements tend to weigh on the currencies of countries that export heavily to the country imposing them, and on global equity indices through growth concerns. The effect on the tariff-imposing country's currency is less predictable: tariffs can reduce imports and support it, but they can also hurt growth and confidence. Treat each episode on its merits and watch how the market actually reacts rather than assuming a textbook outcome.

## Reading the data

Monthly trade-balance releases for the US, China, Japan, Germany, Australia and Canada appear on the Economic calendar, and current-account figures are usually quarterly. On release day these numbers rarely cause big moves because they are slow-moving and partly known in advance. China's trade data is an exception: it is watched as a signal of global demand and can move AUD and commodity markets.

> **Note:** Trade and current-account data work best as background context for multi-week and multi-month views, not as short-term trading signals.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating every deficit as a sell signal.** Funding conditions and investor confidence matter more than the number alone.
- **Expecting instant reactions.** Trade flows shape trends over quarters, not minutes.
- **Forgetting commodities.** For AUDUSD and USDCAD, commodity prices are often the most visible part of the external story.
- **Ignoring vulnerability in risk-off periods.** Large deficit currencies tend to fall further when global risk appetite collapses.
