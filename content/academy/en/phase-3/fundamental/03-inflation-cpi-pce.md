Inflation is the rate at which the general price level rises. It is the number central banks are mandated to control, so inflation releases are among the most market-moving data on the calendar. To trade around them sensibly you need to know which figure the market is actually watching, how to read it, and why a small decimal surprise can move USDJPY by a full yen.

## How inflation is measured

The **Consumer Price Index (CPI)** tracks the cost of a fixed basket of goods and services bought by households: housing, food, transport, medical care, clothing, recreation and so on. Statisticians collect prices each month and weight them by how much households spend on each category. The index level itself is arbitrary; what matters is the percentage change.

In the United States, CPI is published by the Bureau of Labor Statistics, usually around the middle of the month. The Federal Reserve's official target, however, is based on the **Personal Consumption Expenditures (PCE) price index**, released later in the month alongside personal income and spending data. PCE covers a broader range of spending, including items paid on households' behalf such as employer-provided health insurance, and adjusts its weights more quickly when people switch between products. PCE inflation has historically run a little below CPI.

In the eurozone the equivalent is the Harmonised Index of Consumer Prices (HICP), with a flash estimate at the start of the month. The UK publishes CPI monthly and the Bank of England targets it directly.

## Headline versus core

**Headline** inflation includes everything. **Core** inflation excludes food and energy, which are volatile and driven by weather, harvests, geopolitics and oil markets. A spike in petrol prices can push headline inflation up for a few months without telling you much about the underlying trend.

Central banks cannot do much about a drought or an oil supply shock, but they can influence demand-driven inflation in services, wages and rents. That is why markets react more strongly to core figures. Analysts also watch narrower measures such as **services excluding housing** because they are closely linked to wage growth and tend to be persistent.

## Month-on-month versus year-on-year

Each release reports both the change from the previous month (MoM) and the change from the same month a year ago (YoY).

- **YoY** is the headline number the press quotes and the one targets are defined against.
- **MoM** shows the current pace. Markets often react more to the MoM core figure because it is fresher.

```text
Annualising a monthly rate
0.2% per month:  1.002^12 - 1  ≈  2.4% per year
0.3% per month:  1.003^12 - 1  ≈  3.7% per year
0.4% per month:  1.004^12 - 1  ≈  4.9% per year
```

A run of 0.2% monthly prints is broadly consistent with a 2% goal. A run of 0.4% prints is not, even if the YoY number still looks acceptable.

**Base effects** explain why YoY figures can mislead. If energy prices jumped 5% in March last year, this March's YoY rate will drop sharply as that jump leaves the 12-month window, even if nothing changed this month. Knowing which months are dropping out helps you anticipate the YoY direction before the release.

## Why inflation moves markets

The market's question is always the same: what does this number change about the expected path of interest rates?

> **Example:** Consensus for US core CPI is 0.2% MoM. The actual prints 0.4%. Traders conclude the Fed will keep rates higher for longer. Short-term Treasury yields jump, the dollar strengthens (USDJPY might rise from 154.20 to 155.10, a 90-pip move), NAS100 and SPX500 fall as higher rates weigh on valuations, and XAUUSD drops as real yields rise. If core had printed 0.1%, the reactions would typically reverse.

The same logic applies elsewhere. A hot UK CPI tends to support GBPUSD, and a soft eurozone HICP flash tends to weigh on EURUSD. The size of the move depends on how far the surprise is from consensus and how much the central bank is currently focused on inflation. When a central bank has said it is "data dependent" and inflation is the main worry, CPI can be the single biggest event of the month.

> **Risk warning:** Inflation releases often produce fast, two-way moves, wider spreads and slippage in the first seconds. CFDs are leveraged, so losses around these releases can be larger than expected. News-trading technique is covered in Phase 4.

## Common mistakes

- **Reacting to headline when core diverges.** A soft headline caused by cheaper petrol with a hot core figure is usually read as hawkish.
- **Ignoring revisions and details.** Markets dig into components such as shelter and services within minutes. The first spike can reverse once the details are digested.
- **Confusing the level with the surprise.** Inflation of 3.5% YoY is not bullish or bearish on its own; what counts is the comparison with consensus and with the previous trend.
- **Forgetting the other currency.** A hot US CPI can be offset in EURUSD if eurozone inflation is surprising even more.

A useful habit: before each CPI release, write down the consensus for core MoM and decide in advance what you would consider a meaningful surprise, for example 0.1 percentage point either side. It forces you to think in terms of expectations rather than headlines.
