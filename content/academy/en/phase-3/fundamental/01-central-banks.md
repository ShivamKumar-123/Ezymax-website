Every price on your screen is, in the end, a price of money over time. Central banks set the price of short-term money in their currency, and that single number filters into exchange rates, bond yields, stock valuations and the cost of holding gold. If you understand what a central bank is trying to do and how markets anticipate it, a large share of the big moves in forex, indices and metals starts to make sense.

## What central banks want

The major central banks share a core goal: price stability, which in practice means inflation of about 2% per year over the medium term. The European Central Bank (ECB), Bank of England (BoE), Bank of Japan (BoJ), Swiss National Bank (SNB), Bank of Canada and Reserve Bank of Australia all run versions of an inflation-targeting framework. The US Federal Reserve has a **dual mandate**: maximum employment and stable prices, and it measures its 2% goal with the PCE price index.

Why 2% rather than zero? A small positive inflation rate gives room to cut real interest rates in a downturn and reduces the risk of deflation, where falling prices encourage people to delay spending. Too much inflation erodes purchasing power and makes planning harder. The target is a compromise, and central banks will accept short periods away from it if they believe the deviation is temporary.

## The toolkit

- **Policy rate.** The overnight rate the central bank controls, such as the Fed funds target range or the ECB deposit rate. Changes pass through to mortgage rates, business loans and deposit rates.
- **Balance sheet.** Quantitative easing (QE) means buying government bonds to push down longer-term yields. Quantitative tightening (QT) is the reverse: letting bonds mature without reinvesting, or selling them.
- **Forward guidance.** Statements about the likely future path of policy. Guidance often moves markets more than the decision itself.
- **Foreign-exchange intervention.** Buying or selling its own currency. Japan and Switzerland have used this at times; most central banks rarely do.

A central bank that leans towards tighter policy to fight inflation is called **hawkish**. One leaning towards easier policy to support growth is **dovish**.

The chain runs through expectations. When traders expect a central bank to raise rates, short-term bond yields rise first. Higher yields make deposits and bonds in that currency more attractive, so capital tends to flow in and the currency strengthens. For equities, higher rates raise the discount rate applied to future profits and increase borrowing costs, which usually weighs on valuations, especially for growth-heavy indices such as NAS100. Gold pays no interest, so rising real yields raise the opportunity cost of holding it.

## Expectations: the part beginners miss

Markets are forward-looking. Interest-rate futures and swaps price the probability of each outcome well before the meeting. By decision day, a widely expected hike is already reflected in EURUSD or USDJPY. What moves price is the **surprise**: a different decision, a changed vote split, new projections, or a shift in tone at the press conference.

> **Example:** Suppose markets fully price a 0.25% ECB cut. The ECB cuts by 0.25% as expected, but the statement says further cuts are "not a foregone conclusion" and inflation projections are revised up. EURUSD can rise even though rates were cut, because the expected future path of ECB rates has moved higher. The headline decision matched expectations; the guidance did not.

The practical question is always: "Compared with what was priced, is this more hawkish or more dovish?"

## The meeting cycle

The Fed (FOMC), ECB and BoE each hold eight scheduled policy meetings a year. Around each one you will typically see a statement, a press conference, and at some meetings updated economic projections. Minutes released a few weeks later can also move markets. Between meetings, speeches by policymakers shift expectations, particularly when a voter changes tone.

## Common mistakes

- **Trading the headline only.** A hike does not automatically mean a stronger currency. Guidance and projections matter as much as the decision.
- **Ignoring the other side of the pair.** EURUSD reflects both the ECB and the Fed. A hawkish ECB can be outweighed by an even more hawkish Fed.
- **Assuming central banks act on one data point.** They respond to trends in inflation and employment. One hot CPI print moves expectations, but rarely decides policy by itself.
- **Forgetting the calendar.** Holding a leveraged position into a decision without knowing it is scheduled is a common, avoidable source of large losses.
