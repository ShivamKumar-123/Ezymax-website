For the Federal Reserve, maximum employment is half of its mandate, and for every central bank the labour market is a key driver of wages and therefore of services inflation. That makes jobs data some of the most market-moving on the calendar. The US employment report, usually released on the first Friday of the month at 08:30 New York time, is famous among traders simply as NFP.

## What is in the jobs report

The report comes from two separate surveys run by the Bureau of Labor Statistics.

The **establishment survey** asks employers how many people are on their payrolls. It produces:

- **Nonfarm payrolls (NFP):** the monthly change in the number of jobs, excluding farm workers, private household employees and a few other groups.
- **Average hourly earnings (AHE):** wage growth, reported month on month and year on year.
- **Average weekly hours** worked.
- **Revisions** to the two previous months.

The **household survey** asks households about their employment status. It produces:

- **Unemployment rate:** unemployed people actively looking for work, as a share of the labour force.
- **Participation rate:** the share of the working-age population in the labour force.

Because the samples and definitions differ, the two surveys can tell different stories in any single month. The establishment survey is larger and usually considered more reliable for job growth; the household survey is the source of the unemployment rate the Fed quotes.

## Reading the numbers together

A good habit is to read the report in this order:

1. **Headline payrolls versus consensus.** This drives the first second of the reaction.
2. **Revisions.** Downward revisions of 50,000 or more can cancel out a headline beat.
3. **Unemployment rate.** A rise of 0.1 or 0.2 percentage points when unemployment is already trending up is taken seriously.
4. **Average hourly earnings.** A 0.4% monthly wage gain is roughly 4.9% annualised, which is hard to square with a 2% inflation goal; around 0.3% or below is usually seen as consistent with cooling inflation.
5. **Participation and hours.** These explain odd moves in the other figures.

> **Example:** Consensus expects +180,000 jobs, unemployment at 4.1% and AHE +0.3% MoM. The release shows +250,000, unemployment 4.0% and AHE +0.4%, with prior months revised up by 20,000. Every component points to a stronger labour market and firmer wage pressure. Traders price fewer Fed cuts: USD rises, EURUSD might fall from 1.0850 to 1.0790 (60 pips), US30 might drop 250 points from 39,200 to 38,950, and XAUUSD might fall around 25 dollars. Had payrolls beaten while AHE printed 0.1% and unemployment rose, the reaction could easily have been mixed or even reversed.

## Why the labour market matters for policy

Employment affects policy through two channels. First, it is directly part of the Fed's mandate. Second, a tight labour market with more job openings than workers pushes wages up, and wages are the largest cost for service businesses. Fast wage growth therefore tends to keep services inflation high. When unemployment starts rising quickly, central banks become more concerned about recession and more willing to cut.

The same logic applies outside the US. The UK labour market report, with its wage figures, is a major event for GBPUSD, and Canada releases its jobs data on the same Friday as the US, which can make USDCAD especially volatile that morning.

## Other labour indicators

- **Weekly initial jobless claims** (Thursdays) give a timely signal of layoffs. A sustained rise is an early recession warning.
- **JOLTS job openings** measures labour demand and is released with about a one-month lag.
- **ADP private payrolls**, released two days before NFP, is a separate private estimate. Its correlation with the official number month to month is loose, so treat it with caution.

## NFP in practice

The NFP release is notorious for violent, two-directional price action. The first move often reflects only the headline; within 15 to 30 minutes, once traders have digested wages, revisions and the unemployment rate, price can reverse completely. Liquidity is thin in the seconds around the release, spreads widen, and stop orders can be filled well away from their level.

> **Risk warning:** CFDs are leveraged, and jobs-report volatility can cause slippage and losses larger than your planned stop. Many experienced traders reduce size or stay flat through the release. Holding positions through major news is a decision, not a default.

> **In Kalks Trader:** Check the release time in the Economic calendar. If you hold a position into the report, consider whether your stop loss and position size still make sense given that the stop may be filled at a worse price.

## Common mistakes

- **Trading the headline in isolation.** Revisions and wages frequently matter more.
- **Treating ADP as a forecast of NFP.** It is a different measure and often misses.
- **Chasing the first spike.** Initial moves are often retraced as the details are read.
- **Forgetting the policy context.** Strong jobs data is bullish for the dollar mainly when the market is debating how fast the Fed will cut or how long it will hold.
