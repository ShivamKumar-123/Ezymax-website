Central banks control interest rates, but governments control the budget. How much a government spends, how it taxes and how it funds the gap between the two is **fiscal policy**. Most of the time fiscal news moves markets slowly, through growth and bond supply. Occasionally, when investors lose confidence in a government's plans, it becomes the most important story in the market overnight.

## Fiscal versus monetary policy

- **Monetary policy** is set by an independent central bank using interest rates and its balance sheet.
- **Fiscal policy** is set by the government and parliament through spending, taxation and borrowing.

The two interact. A large fiscal stimulus adds demand to the economy, which can raise inflation and force the central bank to keep rates higher. Fiscal austerity reduces demand and can give the central bank room to cut. Traders watch both because the mix matters: loose fiscal policy with tight monetary policy has historically tended to support a currency, as higher rates attract capital while growth holds up.

## Deficits and debt

The **budget balance** is revenue minus spending in a given year. When spending exceeds revenue, the government runs a **deficit** and borrows the difference by issuing bonds. **Government debt** is the stock of all past borrowing still outstanding.

Two further terms are useful:

- The **primary balance** excludes interest payments. It shows whether the government is covering its current spending before servicing old debt.
- **Debt-to-GDP** compares debt with the size of the economy, the standard way to compare countries.

Governments also have **automatic stabilisers**: tax revenue falls and benefit payments rise automatically in a downturn, which widens the deficit and supports demand without any new decision. That is why deficits widen in recessions even if nobody changes policy.

## What makes debt sustainable

A high debt ratio is not automatically a crisis. Japan has carried debt above 200% of GDP for years, largely funded domestically at low rates. What matters is the relationship between the interest rate paid on debt (**r**) and the nominal growth rate of the economy (**g**), together with the primary balance.

```text
Approximate change in debt-to-GDP (one year)
≈ (r - g) × debt ratio  -  primary balance (as % of GDP)

Case 1: debt 100%, r = 3%, g = 5%, primary balance 0%
        (0.03 - 0.05) × 100%  =  -2 points   → ratio falls to about 98%

Case 2: debt 100%, r = 5%, g = 3%, primary deficit 3% of GDP
        (0.05 - 0.03) × 100% + 3%  =  +5 points  → ratio rises to about 105%
```

When interest rates rise above growth, debt dynamics worsen quickly, especially for countries that must refinance large amounts of short-dated debt. This is why a rising interest bill attracts market attention in periods of higher rates.

## How fiscal news moves markets

**Bond supply.** Larger deficits mean more government bonds to sell. If demand does not keep pace, yields rise to attract buyers. Weak demand at a government bond auction can push yields up and move currencies and indices the same day.

**Growth and rates.** Expansionary budgets can lift growth forecasts and rate expectations, supporting the currency in normal conditions.

**Credibility and risk premium.** When investors doubt a government's fiscal plans, they demand a higher yield to hold its bonds and may sell the currency at the same time. This combination, **rising yields with a falling currency**, is the clearest warning sign. It resembles an emerging-market-style loss of confidence rather than a normal rate story.

> **Example:** A government announces large unfunded tax cuts. Within days, 10-year yields rise from 4.20% to 4.70% and the currency falls. GBPUSD, say, drops from 1.2700 to 1.2450, a 250-pip decline, while local equities fall on higher borrowing costs. Normally a 50-basis-point rise in yields would support the currency; here the move reflects a risk premium, not expected central-bank hikes. The sign that something is different is that yields and the currency move against the usual relationship.

**Credit ratings and deadlines.** Downgrades by rating agencies, budget votes, government shutdown deadlines and debt-ceiling standoffs in the US can all produce short bursts of volatility, particularly in USD pairs, US indices and gold, which sometimes benefits as a hedge.

> **Risk warning:** Fiscal shocks can cause large gaps and fast moves, including over weekends when budgets or elections are announced. CFDs are leveraged and losses can exceed expectations; consider your weekend exposure when a major fiscal event is scheduled.

## Common mistakes

- **Equating high debt with an imminent crisis.** The funding structure, interest cost and growth rate matter more than the headline ratio.
- **Assuming higher yields always help a currency.** If yields rise because of fiscal fear, the currency can fall.
- **Ignoring the calendar.** Budget statements, auction results and rating reviews are scheduled; know when they are due.
- **Forgetting the policy mix.** Fiscal loosening can change what the central bank does next, which is often the bigger market driver.
