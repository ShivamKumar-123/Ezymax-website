Inflation tells central banks how hot the economy is running on prices. Growth data tells them how hot it is running on activity. Together they shape the rate outlook and the market's sense of risk. This chapter covers the two growth measures you will see most on the calendar: gross domestic product (GDP) and purchasing managers' indices (PMIs).

## GDP: the broadest measure

Gross domestic product is the total market value of goods and services produced in an economy over a period. The most common way to break it down is by spending:

```text
GDP  =  C  +  I  +  G  +  (X - M)

C = household consumption
I = business and residential investment (plus inventories)
G = government spending on goods and services
X - M = exports minus imports (net exports)
```

In the US, household consumption accounts for roughly two-thirds of GDP, so the health of the consumer is central. That is why retail sales, consumer confidence and jobs data are so closely watched: they are early reads on the biggest part of the economy.

GDP is measured in **real** terms, adjusted for inflation, so that growth reflects more output rather than higher prices.

## Reading a GDP release

Countries report growth differently, and mixing them up leads to wrong conclusions.

- The **US** reports the quarterly change as a **seasonally adjusted annualised rate**. A figure of 2.0% means the economy grew at a pace that would equal 2.0% over a full year.
- The **eurozone** and **UK** usually headline the plain **quarter-on-quarter** change, plus a year-on-year figure.

```text
Converting quarter-on-quarter to annualised
Eurozone +0.5% QoQ:  1.005^4 - 1  ≈  2.0% annualised
UK       +0.2% QoQ:  1.002^4 - 1  ≈  0.8% annualised
```

So eurozone +0.5% and US +2.0% describe a similar pace of growth. US GDP comes in three estimates, advance, second and third, with the advance estimate about a month after the quarter ends. Revisions can be significant.

A **technical recession** is often described as two consecutive quarters of negative GDP growth, although official US recession dating uses a broader set of indicators.

## PMIs: the early warning system

Purchasing managers' indices are monthly surveys of companies asking whether conditions such as output, new orders, employment, prices paid and supplier delivery times are better, the same or worse than the previous month. The answers are combined into a **diffusion index**:

- Above **50**: more firms report improvement than deterioration, indicating expansion.
- Below **50**: contraction.
- The distance from 50 indicates breadth, and the change from last month shows momentum.

The best-known are the S&P Global flash PMIs for the US, eurozone, UK and Japan (released near the end of the month they cover) and the ISM manufacturing and services indices in the US (released in the first days of the following month). Because they arrive so early, PMIs often move EURUSD, GBPUSD and GER40 more than the GDP data that comes months later.

Sub-components carry extra information. **New orders** hint at future output. **Prices paid** feeds straight into inflation expectations. **Employment** gives a preview of jobs data.

> **Example:** The eurozone flash composite PMI is expected at 50.5 and prints 48.9, with new orders falling to 47.2. Traders see rising odds of ECB cuts. EURUSD might drop from 1.0850 to 1.0815 (35 pips) within an hour, while GER40 may hold up better than you might expect, because lower rate expectations partly offset weaker growth. Markets weigh several channels at once.

## How growth data moves markets

Stronger-than-expected growth usually lifts the currency, because it supports higher rates, and helps equities through better earnings expectations. But the relationship is not linear. If growth is strong while inflation is already above target, traders may fear more tightening, so indices can fall on good news. When inflation is under control and recession fears dominate, weak data can hurt equities and help safe havens such as JPY, CHF and gold.

Always ask where the central bank's attention is. When the focus is inflation, growth data matters mainly through its effect on prices. When the focus is recession risk, growth data takes centre stage.

> **Risk warning:** Growth releases can trigger fast moves and wider spreads. CFDs are leveraged and losses can exceed expectations. Use the Economic calendar to know when releases are due before you hold positions through them.

## In practice

- Track the monthly PMIs for the economies behind the pairs you trade; they build the growth picture long before GDP confirms it.
- Compare GDP figures only after converting them to the same basis.
- Pay attention to revisions and components, not only the headline.
- Consider the regime: in an inflation-worry phase, "good news" for growth can be bad news for indices.

Growth and inflation data together form the core of the central-bank reaction function. The next chapter adds the third pillar that the Fed in particular treats as part of its mandate: the labour market.
